The Circle in the Sky: Why the Yurt Crown Is on Kyrgyzstan’s Flag

2026/10/032 min readGo2Mountains

Lie on your back inside a Kyrgyz yurt and look straight up. Above you, where all the roof poles meet, is a wooden ring crossed by curved bars, with a circle of sky in the middle. That ring is the tündük, and to the Kyrgyz it means far more than a hole for the smoke.

The window to the sky

Every yurt is held together at the top by its crown. The roof poles slot into the ring, the felt cover is pulled back around it, and through the opening comes daylight, fresh air and, at night, the stars. It’s the only window a traditional yurt has, and nomads could read the time of day from the patch of sunlight moving across the floor.

A home you inherit

A yurt’s lattice walls and felt can be repaired or replaced over the years. The tündük is different. It is the heart of the home, and traditionally it was passed down within the family, often staying in the family home with the youngest son, who looked after his parents.

To say “the tündük still stands” is to say a family line continues. A family’s tündük could be generations old, darkened by decades of smoke, and treated with deep respect.

Look closely: in many yurts you’ll find the crown wrapped with colourful ribbons or decorated bands. It’s the most honoured part of the home.

Why it’s on the flag

When Kyrgyzstan chose a national flag after independence in 1991, it put a yurt crown inside a golden sun on a red field. The message is simple: a shared home under one sky, and a people whose roots are in nomadic life.

So the next time you see the flag flying in Bishkek, you’ll know you’re looking at a view from inside a yurt.

How a yurt comes together

If you join a yurt-building session on one of our tours, you’ll see the order for yourself:

  • Kerege: the folding lattice walls are opened out into a circle.
  • Door frame: set into the wall, traditionally facing the morning sun.
  • Tündük: raised into the centre on poles, the hardest and most exciting moment.
  • Uuk: the curved roof poles are slotted between the walls and the crown.
  • Felt: thick felt covers go on, tied down with long woven bands.

An experienced family can put up a yurt in an hour or two. A group of first-timers takes a little longer, and laughs a lot more.

On the south shore of Issyk-Kul, in the yurt-making village of Kyzyl-Tuu, you can help raise a tündük yourself. At Song-Kul, you can fall asleep looking through one at the stars.
