Manas: The Epic Kyrgyz Storytellers Recite From Memory

2026/10/032 min readGo2Mountains

Imagine a story so long that no one ever wrote it down for centuries, yet people knew it by heart and could perform it for days. In Kyrgyzstan, that story exists. It is called Manas.

The hero who united the tribes

The epic tells of Manas, a legendary warrior who united the scattered Kyrgyz tribes and led them in defending their land. It is followed by the stories of his son Semetei and grandson Seitek, so the whole cycle is often called the Manas trilogy.

But Manas is much more than battles. It is an encyclopaedia of nomadic life: how people moved, married, mourned, raised horses, held feasts and settled arguments. For a culture that lived on horseback, the epic was its history book, law book and evening entertainment all at once.

One of the longest poems in the world

Some recorded versions of Manas run to around half a million lines. That’s many times longer than the Iliad and the Odyssey together. Because it was passed on orally, there is no single “correct” version: each great storyteller had his own.

The manaschi

The people who perform the epic are called manaschi. A manaschi doesn’t read; he recites from memory, chanting in a rising, rhythmic voice, often with closed eyes and sweeping hand gestures. Traditionally, many manaschi said the epic came to them in a dream, and that they were chosen to carry it.

Recognised by UNESCO: in 2013, the Kyrgyz epic trilogy Manas, Semetey, Seytek was inscribed on UNESCO’s list of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

Where you’ll meet Manas today

Once you know the name, you’ll see it everywhere:

  • Manas International Airport in Bishkek, where most visitors land.
  • The Manas statue on Ala-Too Square, in the centre of the capital.
  • Streets, schools, a university and even a peak named after the hero.
  • Children who learn passages at school, and adults who can still quote them.

Hearing a manaschi

A live performance, even a short one, is unforgettable: you won’t understand the words, but you’ll feel the rhythm and the emotion of the room. Performances happen at festivals, cultural events and some museums.

Ask us when you plan your trip, and we’ll tell you where you might hear Manas during your dates. Some stories are best experienced the way they were always meant to be: out loud.
